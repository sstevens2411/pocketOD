Toric Contact Lens Fitting Guide for Optometrists

Toric contact lenses correct astigmatism by delivering a cylindrical power at a precise axis, held in place by a built-in stabilization mechanism. Successful fitting requires matching cylinder power and axis, assessing on-eye orientation at the slit lamp, and applying a compensated axis when rotation is present.

Key Points
  • Fit toric when cylinder is ≥0.75 D; consider at 0.50 D for symptomatic patients dissatisfied with spherical correction
  • Apply vertex conversion before selecting sphere when spectacle Rx exceeds ±4.00 D
  • LARS — Left Add, Right Subtract — compensates for on-eye axis rotation; reliable only when rotation is stable
  • Wait 10–15 min after insertion before assessing lens orientation
  • Unstable rotation usually means a flat fit — steepen BC or switch stabilization design

When to Fit a Toric Contact Lens

  • Fit toric when refractive cylinder is ≥0.75 D. Below this, a spherical lens at the spherical equivalent usually suffices. Consider toric at 0.50 D for symptomatic patients with higher visual demands.
  • Confirm astigmatism is refractive, not purely lenticular. A significant discrepancy between corneal toricity and refractive cylinder warrants further evaluation — a corneal toric lens does not correct lenticular astigmatism.
  • Irregular corneal astigmatism (keratoconus, Salzmann's nodular degeneration, corneal scarring) is not reliably corrected by soft torics — a rigid or scleral lens is usually more appropriate.

Rotation Assessment & the LARS Rule

Most soft torics carry an orientation mark near the inferior lens edge at 6 o'clock. After 10–15 minutes, assess the mark under the slit lamp. A mark displaced toward the patient's right indicates leftward (counterclockwise) rotation; toward the left indicates rightward (clockwise) rotation.

LARS — Left Add, Right Subtract:

On-eye rotationRuleExample: spectacle axis 165°, trialed at 160°
Left (counterclockwise)Add degrees of rotation to ordered axis15° left → 160° + 15° = 175°
Right (clockwise)Subtract degrees of rotation from ordered axis10° right → 160° − 10° = 150°

LARS is reliable only when rotation is stable and reproducible across multiple blinks. If the axis shifts >10–15° between observations, address the fitting problem first — a different base curve, stabilization design, or lens family — before applying LARS.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much astigmatism warrants a toric contact lens?
Most clinicians use 0.75 D of refractive cylinder as the threshold. Below that, a spherical lens at the spherical equivalent usually suffices. Symptomatic patients with higher visual demands may benefit from toric correction at 0.50 D, particularly if they are unsatisfied with their current spherical lens.

What is the LARS rule?
LARS stands for Left Add, Right Subtract. If the toric lens rotates to the patient's left (counterclockwise), add the degrees of rotation to the spectacle axis when ordering. If it rotates right (clockwise), subtract. This compensates for the axis shift introduced by lens rotation and is reliable only when rotation is stable and consistent.

How long should I wait before assessing rotation?
Allow at least 10–15 minutes for the lens to equilibrate after insertion. Immediately post-insertion, lens position is influenced by residual fluid and handling artifact. Assessing too early can misrepresent the settled on-eye orientation and lead to an unnecessary axis compensation.

What causes a toric lens to rotate excessively?
A flat base curve is the most common cause — the lens moves too freely when it does not conform closely to the corneal curvature. Lid anatomy that does not engage the stabilization zone effectively is another factor. Steepening the base curve (if available) or switching to a lens with a different stabilization design are the primary interventions.

Can I apply LARS if lens rotation is variable?
No. LARS only works when rotation is stable and repeatable across blinks. If the axis shifts by more than 10–15° between observations, the fit itself needs to be corrected first. Applying LARS to an unstable lens produces an inconsistent result that does not reliably improve acuity.
